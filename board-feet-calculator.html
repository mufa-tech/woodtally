Why the rules disagree

Doyle under-scales small logs

Doyle takes 4 inches off the diameter for slabs, then squares what is left: (D − 4)² × L ÷ 16. On a 12-inch log those 4 inches are a third of the width, so a 12 in × 16 ft log scales 64 bf by Doyle and 95 bf by International ¼. The gap closes near 28 inches, and on big logs Doyle comes out ahead.

Scribner is a drawing, not a formula

J. M. Scribner drew 1-inch boards inside circles, allowed a ¼-inch saw kerf, ignored taper and wrote down the totals. Scribner Decimal C rounds those totals to tens and drops the last zero, so a table value of 16 means 160 bf. The table jumps unevenly, so any formula is only an approximation, and the common shortcut is off by 10 % or more on some logs (14 in × 20 ft: about 154 bf against 140 in the table). That is why this page does not compute it. Woodtally uses the USFS table.

International ¼ follows the log

International ¼ assumes the taper real logs have, ½ inch every 4 feet, and allows for a ¼-inch kerf and shrinkage. Of the three it comes closest to what a modern mill saws. This page adds up Clark's 4-foot sections, 0.22 D² − 0.71 D, with D growing ½ inch per section, multiplies by 0.905 for the ¼-inch kerf and rounds to the nearest 5 bf. The printed table rounds up in a few cells (15 in × 16 ft is 160 in the table and 155 here). The app uses the table.

What it means when you sell

A load of 12-inch logs can lose a third of its footage on Doyle compared with International ¼. Ask which rule the buyer scales by before you agree a price per thousand, and run a few logs on more than one rule.

How to measure

Diameter at the small end, inside the bark, two readings at right angles, averaged, fractions dropped. Length in whole feet without the trim allowance. Logs over 20 feet are scaled as two logs.
